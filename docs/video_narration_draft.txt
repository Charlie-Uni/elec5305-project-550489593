00:00–00:45 | Listen to a competing event
Visual: project page, can opening, same-category event, +5 dB. Keep the player
volume identical. Play the five-second gain-matched control, then the mixture.

Narration:
This project asks what happens when background interference is itself a
recognisable sound. The first recording is a can opening. The second contains
the same target and a clock alarm, with the target five decibels stronger under
the declared activity-power convention. In this selected example, the CNN and
CLAP identify the clean control correctly, then both predict the clock alarm
in the mixture. The SVM instead predicts chirping birds. This illustrates two
different kinds of failure: a competing event can capture the decision, or the
prediction can move to another class entirely. The experiment measures how
often each happens across the full dataset, rather than drawing a conclusion
from this single example.

00:45–01:35 | Research question and three representations
Visual: three system descriptions on the project page; keep the full research
question and the external-pretraining caveat visible.

Narration:
The central question is how different audio representations respond to ordinary
acoustic noise and a competing meaningful event at controlled relative levels.
The first system is a classical SVM using 92 temporal, spectral and MFCC features.
The second is a compact CNN trained on log-Mel spectrograms. The third is frozen
LAION-CLAP, which compares an audio embedding with text descriptions of the fifty
classes. CLAP is not trained or fine-tuned here. It uses one fixed prompt template
in the core experiment. These systems have very different training histories,
so this is a study of failure patterns and deployment behaviour, rather than an
equal-data contest to determine the best model.

01:35–02:25 | DSP and experimental controls
Visual: report methods and mixture-manifest example, followed by the official
five-fold evaluation description. Use a readable crop, not a whole dense page.

Narration:
I use all two thousand ESC-50 clips and the five official test folds. For each
outer fold, three folds select the supervised model with a separate validation
fold; a fresh model is then fitted using all four non-test folds. Normalisation
is learned from the relevant training data. Semantic distractors come from the
held-out test fold, with different labels and source recordings. Pairings remain
fixed across models and interference levels. The acoustic controls are white
Gaussian noise and three DEMAND environments. Every target is evaluated at plus
ten, plus five and zero decibels. The same noise realisation is retained across
levels. Scaling is verified numerically, and common attenuation prevents clipping.
Every mixture also has a clean target control with exactly the same attenuation.

02:25–03:15 | Why power convention and overlap matter
Visual: website method explanations, then overlap-stratification figure. Show
own/target definitions together; do not suggest one replaces the other.

Narration:
The original proposal measured both components over the target's active region.
With a sparse distractor, there may be almost no interference energy in that
region, which can demand an extreme scaling gain. The implemented default
instead measures each signal over its own active region. That is a documented
method change, so I ran both definitions as a sensitivity comparison. Three
pairs have zero interference energy in the target region and cannot satisfy a
finite requested ratio; their nine conditions are explicitly excluded from
that protocol. Neither power convention guarantees temporal overlap. A competing
event that mostly occurs during target silence is a different challenge from
one that sounds simultaneously. The analysis therefore also compares matched
high-overlap targets. Zero decibels is treated as an ambiguous-label stress test.

03:15–04:10 | Main robustness results
Visual: macro-F1 curves, followed by the +5 dB table. Point to one system at a
time. Values below refer to the own-activity protocol and all retained targets.

Narration:
The clean five-fold macro-F1 means are about forty-eight percent for the SVM,
sixty-seven percent for the CNN and seventy-nine percent for CLAP. At plus five
decibels, white noise reduces SVM macro-F1 to about two percent, while its scores
under semantic interference remain around twenty-one to twenty-two percent.
CLAP shows a different pattern: roughly seventy-one percent with DEMAND, but
about forty-nine and forty-five percent with same-category and different-category
events. Thus interference power alone does not describe the failure pattern.
For the CNN, aggregate semantic scores look slightly better than DEMAND, but
the comparison changes when attention is restricted to strongly overlapping
pairs. Both the overall results and the stratified results are reported, because
they answer different questions about the mixture population.

04:10–05:10 | Where the decision moves
Visual: capture-rate curves, target–distractor margins and CLAP distractor-rank
plot. Do not show all three figures at a size too small to read.

Narration:
Accuracy alone cannot show whether the model recognises the interfering event.
Distractor capture rate is the fraction of incorrect predictions that equal
the distractor label. At plus five decibels, about sixty to sixty-seven percent
of CLAP's errors under semantic interference name that distractor. The rate is
lower for the supervised systems. I also retain the unconditional capture
fraction, because the conditional rate depends on how many errors a model makes.
The target-minus-distractor margin shows how their relative scores change as
the interferer strengthens. These score scales are compared within a model,
not between architectures. For CLAP, the distractor's median rank among all
fifty classes rises to approximately third at plus five decibels and second at
zero decibels. This provides evidence of structured competition rather than
merely recording a drop in the correct-class score.

05:10–06:00 | Representation diagnostics and uncertainty
Visual: feature-group heatmap and paired high-overlap accuracy intervals.

Narration:
White noise strongly changes the SVM's zero-crossing and spectral descriptors.
The group analysis separates those changes from MFCCs, their deltas and energy.
For the CNN, smaller embedding displacement does not necessarily imply better
classification: semantic interference can cause more errors on high-overlap
targets despite less overall movement than DEMAND. This association does not
isolate a causal effect of meaning, because the sounds also differ acoustically.
Paired comparisons retain the same target recordings. On high-overlap targets,
CLAP loses approximately twenty-six to twenty-nine accuracy points more under
semantic interference than under DEMAND. The core CNN difference is about three
points, but is tentative after multiple-comparison correction. The intervals
condition on fitted models; repeated predictions of one clip are not independent
samples. Training-seed variability is investigated separately.

06:45–07:35 | Computational cost and limitations
Visual: CPU-cost figure and a short limitations view from the report.

Narration:
The compact CNN has about thirty-six thousand parameters. The core batch-one
CPU microbenchmark, including preprocessing, is about three milliseconds per
clip for the CNN, eleven for the SVM and ninety-eight for CLAP. These are
single-clip measurements on the same machine, not a claim about all deployment
hardware. Cached text embeddings exclude repeated prompt preparation from
CLAP's audio-query timing. Its external pretraining cost is not local inference
cost. Limitations include one semantic pairing of each type per target, only
three real background environments, sparse temporal overlap, official source
reuse across a small number of folds, and possible CLAP pretraining overlap.
Equal active power does not make spectral or perceptual difficulty equal.

07:35–08:00 | Conclusion and reproducibility
Visual: project site with report, source, result-table and listening links.

Narration:
The contribution is a controlled signal-processing experiment that distinguishes
ordinary acoustic corruption from competition between recognisable events.
Reproducible mixtures, gain-matched controls, two power conventions and explicit
failure destinations make the comparison interpretable. The repository includes
the DSP and evaluation code, verified tables, figure source data and attributed
audio examples. The report documents method changes and statistical limits so
that the conclusions can be checked rather than inferred from selected clips.
